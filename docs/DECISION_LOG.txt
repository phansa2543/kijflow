# DEC-001 — Phase 1 SaaS Foundation Scope

## Original Date

2026-10-07

## Revision Date

2026-10-10

## Decision

Phase 1 will build the minimum self-service SaaS Foundation required to support a real Organization and Membership lifecycle before the first Business Module.

Phase 1 must remain intentionally limited.

The goal is not to build a complete SaaS platform or enterprise account-management system.

## Context

The original DEC-001 established that Phase 1 should build only the minimum Foundation required before the first Business Module.

A later Product Foundation Review identified that the original scope was technically sufficient but did not fully represent the minimum lifecycle expected from a real self-service SaaS product.

The revised Product Model requires:

- global User Identity
- Membership between Users and Organizations
- self-service User registration
- self-service Organization creation
- Organization ownership
- minimum Membership lifecycle behavior

Billing, packaging, Business Modules, and enterprise functionality remain outside Phase 1.

## Problem

What is the minimum SaaS Foundation Kijflow needs before the first Business Module so that the system represents realistic SaaS identity, membership, ownership, security, and tenant behavior without introducing unnecessary complexity?

## Options Considered

1. Preserve the original minimal technical Foundation without adding real SaaS lifecycle behavior.
2. Expand Phase 1 into a broad SaaS platform including billing, advanced authorization, and enterprise account structures.
3. Expand only the Foundation concepts required for realistic self-service SaaS identity, Organization, Membership, ownership, and security behavior.

## Selected Option

Use option 3.

Build the minimum self-service SaaS Foundation required for a realistic Organization and Membership lifecycle while continuing to defer unrelated SaaS platform and Business Module functionality.

### In Scope

- Global Kijflow User Identity
- Organization
- Membership
- Self-service User registration
- Self-service Organization creation
- Authentication
- Trusted Organization context
- Minimum Membership lifecycle
- Invitation-based Membership onboarding
- Owner / Admin / Member Foundation authorization profiles
- Organization ownership protection and transfer
- Minimal capability-oriented authorization
- Strong tenant/data isolation
- Security and lifecycle behavior required to support the above

### Non-Goals

- Billing / subscription / package implementation
- Payment-provider integration
- Advanced permission builder
- Customer-defined roles
- Direct per-user permission overrides
- Complex approval hierarchy
- Enterprise SSO
- Teams / Departments without validated requirements
- Parent/subsidiary hierarchy
- Group-company hierarchy
- Consolidated multi-company reporting
- Shared inventory across Organizations
- Shared business data across Organizations
- Inter-company transactions
- Custom workflow engine
- Business Modules
- Production-scale DevOps platform
- Data-retention purge implementation
- Customer-offboarding export implementation

## Reasoning

A minimal SaaS Foundation must be small enough for a one-person software house while still representing real SaaS behavior correctly.

Identity, Membership, Organization ownership, and trusted tenant context are foundational concepts.

Deferring them until after Business Modules exist would make future changes materially more expensive across:

- data modeling
- authentication
- authorization
- tenant isolation
- APIs
- UX
- testing
- historical attribution

At the same time, Phase 1 must not expand into billing, multi-company ERP functionality, enterprise IAM, or speculative platform capabilities.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Foundation reflects realistic SaaS usage.
- A User can participate in multiple Organizations without duplicate identities.
- Tenant boundaries remain explicit.
- Membership and ownership lifecycle semantics exist before Business Modules depend on them.
- Future architectural migration risk is reduced.
- Self-service SaaS behavior is supported from the Foundation.

### Sacrifices

- Phase 1 identity and security modeling is more complex than the original one-User/one-Organization model.
- Additional lifecycle and security testing is required.
- Active Organization context must be handled deliberately.
- Phase 1 implementation scope increases moderately.

## Risks

- Membership and Organization switching may create tenant-isolation vulnerabilities if implemented inconsistently.
- Phase 1 could expand unnecessarily if future lifecycle concepts are treated as current implementation requirements.
- Owner semantics could become incorrectly coupled to future Business Module permissions.
- Foundation lifecycle logic could become too complex for one-person maintenance if unnecessary flexibility is introduced.

## Reversibility

Moderate.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider this scope if:

- implementation begins accumulating functionality not required for the accepted Foundation lifecycle,
- validated Business Module requirements reveal missing Foundation capabilities,
- or Foundation complexity becomes disproportionate to the needs of the first Business Module.

- Founder
- Product Manager
- Tech Lead / Software Architect
- Engineering Mentor
- QA / Quality Engineer
- Command Center

# DEC-002 — Phase 1 Identity, Organization, and Membership Model

## Original Date

2026-10-07

## Revision Date

2026-10-10

## Superseded Assumption

The original DEC-002 rule that one User belongs to exactly one Organization is superseded.

Organization remains the tenant/data-isolation boundary.

## Decision

For Phase 1:

- User represents a global Kijflow identity.
- Organization represents the SME/business using Kijflow.
- Organization is the tenant/data-isolation boundary.
- Tenant is not modeled as a separate business/domain entity.
- User and Organization are related through Membership.
- One User may have Memberships in multiple Organizations.
- One Organization may have multiple Memberships.
- A User may have at most one Membership for the same Organization.
- Memberships in different Organizations are independent.
- A User may exist with zero usable Organization Memberships.
- Team and Department are not part of the Phase 1 model.

Multi-Organization Membership does not imply multi-company business functionality.

## Context

The original DEC-002 intentionally selected a one-User/one-Organization model to minimize complexity.

The later Product Foundation Review established a real product requirement for:

- global User Identity
- participation in multiple Organizations
- independent Membership lifecycle
- self-service creation of additional Organizations

The original one-Organization assumption therefore became invalid.

Organization itself remains an appropriate tenant/data-isolation boundary.

## Problem

How should global User identity, Organization participation, and tenant boundaries relate while supporting real SaaS usage without introducing multi-company enterprise functionality?

## Options Considered

1. Keep one User permanently tied to one Organization.
2. Duplicate User accounts when the same person needs another Organization.
3. Use a global User Identity related to Organizations through Membership.
4. Introduce a higher-level Account/Tenant containing several Organizations.

## Selected Option

Use option 3.

Use global User Identity + Organization Membership while keeping each Organization as an independent tenant boundary.

## Conceptual Model

```text
User Identity
     │
     │ 0..N
     ▼
 Membership
     │
     │ N..1
     ▼
Organization
 = Tenant Boundary
```

## Membership Independence

Membership state in one Organization must not implicitly affect Membership in another Organization.

Examples:

- disabling Membership in Organization A does not disable Membership in Organization B,
- revoking Membership in Organization A does not revoke Membership in Organization B,
- leaving Organization A does not delete the global User Identity,
- a User may remain active in Organization B after leaving Organization A.

Organization manages Membership.

Organization does not own the User's global Identity.

## Membership Lifecycle Direction

Phase 1 product semantics must be able to represent:

- Pending
- Active
- Disabled
- Revoked / Former

The exact technical representation of invitation and Pending state is deferred to implementation design.

A Pending invitation does not require creation of an incomplete or fake global User Identity.

## Organization Context

Every tenant-scoped operation must operate within exactly one trusted Organization context.

A User may have access to multiple Organizations, but one tenant-scoped operation is always evaluated against one Organization boundary.

The exact technical representation of active Organization context is deferred to implementation design.

The architecture does not require a globally persisted `User.active_organization_id`.

## Security Constraints

- Client-supplied Organization identifiers do not independently establish trusted Organization context.
- Membership is security-sensitive.
- Normal access to an Organization requires a usable Membership.
- Membership lifecycle changes must not alter Membership in another Organization.
- Tenant-owned business data remains owned by exactly one Organization.
- Global User Identity must not be treated as belonging to whichever Organization is currently active.
- Pre-tenant access to Membership information must be explicitly constrained.

## Historical Identity

Revoking Membership must not destroy global User Identity.

Historical records may continue to identify a former member.

Historical attribution must not depend on the Membership remaining active.

## Non-Goals

This model does not introduce:

- parent/subsidiary hierarchy
- group-company hierarchy
- consolidated reporting
- shared business data across Organizations
- inter-company transactions
- shared inventory
- Teams
- Departments

## Reasoning

Global Identity + Membership reflects real SaaS behavior better than binding a User permanently to one Organization.

It supports:

- owners of multiple businesses
- consultants
- employees participating in different Organizations
- future SaaS use cases requiring the same identity in multiple Organizations

without introducing multi-company ERP semantics.

Organization remains a simple and understandable tenant boundary.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Identity is independent from Organization participation.
- Multi-Organization access does not require duplicate User accounts.
- Membership lifecycle becomes explicit.
- Leaving one Organization does not destroy unrelated access.
- Historical User identity remains stable.
- Organization remains a clear isolation boundary.

### Sacrifices

- Active Organization context becomes necessary.
- Organization switching introduces additional security paths.
- Authorization becomes Membership-specific.
- Membership lifecycle introduces additional data and testing complexity.

## Risks

- Stale Membership or Organization context could expose data if validation is weak.
- Developers could accidentally treat authentication as proof of Organization access.
- Membership discovery could become an unintended cross-tenant enumeration path.
- Duplicate or conflicting Membership state could create authorization ambiguity.

## Reversibility

Moderate to difficult once Business Modules depend on the model.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider if:

- Organization no longer represents the correct tenant boundary,
- one logical tenant must contain multiple business Organizations,
- group-company functionality becomes a validated requirement,
- or Membership semantics become insufficient for validated customer workflows.

## Participants

- Founder
- Product Manager
- Tech Lead / Software Architect
- Engineering Mentor
- QA / Quality Engineer
- Command Center

## Founder Approval

Accepted

# DEC-003 — Phase 1 Foundation Authorization Model

## Original Date

2026-10-08

## Revision Date

2026-10-10

## Superseded Assumption

The original two-profile model:

- Organization Admin
- Member

and the assumption that a distinct Owner profile was unnecessary are superseded.

## Decision

Phase 1 uses three Kijflow-defined Foundation authorization profiles:

- Owner
- Admin
- Member

The Foundation profile belongs to Membership context.

Each usable Membership has exactly one Foundation profile.

A User may therefore hold different profiles in different Organizations.

Organizations cannot create custom profiles or customer-defined roles in Phase 1.

Users cannot receive direct per-user permission overrides.

A configurable customer-defined Role entity is not required.

## Context

The original DEC-003 intentionally used only Admin and Member.

The Product Foundation Review introduced genuine Organization ownership semantics including:

- one Owner per Organization
- ownership transfer
- protection against Admin removal
- Organization lifecycle authority

This created a validated need for Owner in the Foundation authorization model.

Multi-Organization Membership also means authorization must be evaluated per Membership rather than globally per User.

## Problem

What is the minimum Foundation authorization model that supports ownership, administration, Membership-specific authority, and future capability-oriented Business Module authorization without introducing configurable RBAC?

## Options Considered

1. Keep Admin/Member and model ownership separately through ad-hoc flags.
2. Use Owner/Admin/Member as small Kijflow-defined Foundation profiles.
3. Introduce fully configurable Organization roles.
4. Introduce direct per-user permission assignment.
5. Build a generic authorization policy engine.

## Selected Option

Use option 2.

Use three small Kijflow-defined Foundation profiles:

- Owner
- Admin
- Member

Profiles exist within Membership context.

## Example

```text
Organization A → Owner
Organization B → Member
Organization C → Admin
```

## Owner

Owner represents Organization ownership and lifecycle authority.

An active Organization has exactly one Owner.

The Owner:

- must have an active Membership in the Organization,
- is established through trusted system-controlled behavior,
- has ownership/lifecycle authority,
- may transfer ownership,
- cannot be removed through ordinary Admin Membership management,
- cannot be disabled or revoked by ordinary Admin Membership management,
- cannot leave the Organization without first transferring ownership.

Owner does not represent universal Business Module authority.

Owner is not equivalent to:

```text
allow everything
```

## Admin

Admin represents Foundation administrative responsibility.

Admin may perform Foundation administrative actions according to explicit capability policy.

Admin:

- is scoped to the relevant Organization,
- cannot modify the Owner through ordinary Membership management,
- does not bypass tenant isolation,
- is not a universal Business Module superuser.

## Member

Member represents an ordinary Organization participant.

Member authority is determined by explicit capability policy.

## Authorization Scope

Authorization is evaluated within a specific Organization Membership context.

Authentication of a global User does not by itself grant authority inside any Organization.

Conceptually:

```text
Authenticated User
AND usable Membership
AND trusted Organization context
AND required authority
→ operation may proceed
```

## Authorization Principles

- Profile names are not universal authorization bypasses.
- Authorization remains capability/action oriented.
- Owner does not automatically inherit every possible capability.
- Admin does not automatically inherit every possible capability.
- Profiles are not assumed to form an implicit hierarchy.
- Tenant isolation remains independent from authorization.
- A Foundation profile never expands the tenant boundary.

## Lifecycle and Security Invariants

- An active Organization must have exactly one Owner.
- The Owner must have an active Membership.
- Arbitrary client input cannot grant Owner authority.
- Arbitrary client input cannot grant Admin authority.
- Foundation-profile changes are security-sensitive operations.
- Ordinary account/profile updates cannot elevate Foundation authority.
- Ownership transfer must not produce zero Owners.
- Ownership transfer must not produce more than one Owner.
- Ordinary Admin Membership-management operations cannot remove, disable, revoke, or demote the Owner.
- Once a privilege-reducing change takes effect in authoritative state, subsequent authorization decisions must no longer recognize the removed privilege.

## Non-Goals

Phase 1 does not introduce:

- customer-defined roles
- direct per-user permission overrides
- Sales Manager
- Inventory Manager
- Accountant
- configurable RBAC
- general-purpose ABAC
- generic policy engines

Future Kijflow-defined Business Module roles may be introduced only when validated Business Module workflows justify them.

## Reasoning

Owner now has validated Organization ownership and lifecycle semantics.

Using a small fixed set of Foundation profiles keeps the Phase 1 model understandable, testable, and maintainable.

Capability-oriented authorization prevents Owner and Admin from becoming universal future Business Module superusers.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Clear Organization ownership semantics.
- Small Foundation authorization model.
- Different authority per Organization is naturally supported.
- Owner lifecycle protection is explicit.
- No customer-facing RBAC complexity.

### Sacrifices

- Three profiles must be maintained instead of two.
- Ownership transfer introduces concurrency and lifecycle rules.
- Business Module authorization remains intentionally unresolved.

## Risks

- Developers may treat Owner as a universal superuser.
- Profile-name checks may spread through Business Modules.
- Ownership-transfer concurrency may produce invalid state if not enforced correctly.
- Owner/Admin capability mappings could grow excessively if Business Module concerns leak into the Foundation.

## Reversibility

Moderate.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider if:

- validated Business Modules need materially different authority structures,
- Organizations require customer-defined roles,
- per-user exceptions become common,
- segregation of duties becomes necessary,
- resource ownership becomes a frequent authorization dimension,
- contextual/workflow authorization becomes common,
- or Foundation profile mappings become too large or difficult to understand.

## Participants

- Founder
- Product Manager
- Tech Lead / Software Architect
- Engineering Mentor
- QA / Quality Engineer
- Command Center

## Founder Approval

Accepted

# DEC-004 — Phase 1 Permission Architecture

## Original Date

2026-10-08

## Revalidation Date

2026-10-10

## Decision

The core DEC-004 architecture remains accepted.

Kijflow uses a hybrid, capability-oriented permission architecture.

Capabilities are divided by responsibility:

- SaaS Foundation owns capabilities related to Foundation responsibilities.
- Business Modules own capabilities arising from validated business responsibilities and workflows.

Capability definitions and mappings are defined by Kijflow.

Organizations cannot create, modify, or override capability mappings in Phase 1.

Concrete Business Module capability catalogs remain deferred until their requirements are validated.

## Context

DEC-003 now defines three Foundation authorization profiles:

- Owner
- Admin
- Member

This changes the profile mappings used by DEC-004 but does not invalidate the capability-oriented architecture.

The capability architecture remains necessary to prevent Foundation profiles from becoming universal Business Module authorization rules.

## Problem

How should permissions remain secure, understandable, modular, and maintainable after introducing Membership-specific Owner/Admin/Member Foundation profiles?

## Options Considered

1. Abandon capabilities and authorize directly from profile names.
2. Preserve capability-oriented authorization and revise mappings for Owner/Admin/Member.
3. Introduce configurable RBAC.
4. Introduce direct per-user permissions.

## Selected Option

Use option 2.

Preserve the existing hybrid capability architecture and revise only the Foundation profile context and mappings.

## Revised Foundation Profile Context

Phase 1 Foundation capability mappings apply to:

- Owner
- Admin
- Member

Authorization is evaluated within the relevant Membership and Organization context.

## Authorization Invariants

- Every named capability must produce a deterministic outcome for every supported Foundation profile.
- An explicit grant authorizes a named capability.
- Absence of an explicit grant results in denial.
- Missing capability mapping results in denial.
- Unknown capability results in denial.
- Named capabilities fail closed by default.
- Owner does not implicitly bypass capability evaluation.
- Admin does not implicitly bypass capability evaluation.
- Foundation profiles are not assumed to form an implicit permission hierarchy.
- Capability mappings are explicit Kijflow-defined product/security policy.
- A capability never expands the tenant boundary.

## Capability Granularity

Capabilities should represent meaningful distinctions such as:

- administrative responsibility
- business responsibility
- security sensitivity
- materially different business consequences
- materially different audit significance

Capabilities should not mechanically mirror:

- CRUD operations
- API endpoints
- database tables

Two actions may remain separate capabilities even if their current mappings are identical when their business or security meaning differs.

Not every allowed action requires a named capability.

## Ownership-Specific Rules

Some genuine Organization ownership/lifecycle actions may use explicit ownership semantics.

Example:

- transfer Organization ownership

Ownership-specific rules must be:

- explicit
- intentional
- documented
- testable
- limited to genuine ownership/lifecycle behavior

Ownership semantics must not become a hidden bypass for Business Module capabilities.

## Baseline Authorization Rules

Some inherent Foundation behavior may exist without a named capability.

Such behavior must be:

- explicit
- intentional
- documented
- testable

Baseline behavior must never bypass a named capability protecting the same action.

## Capability Ownership

Every named capability must have a clear conceptual owner.

- Foundation capabilities belong to the SaaS Foundation.
- Business-specific capabilities belong to the responsible Business Module.

Cross-module/shared capability ownership remains deferred until module boundaries are established.

## Mapping Changes

Profile-to-capability mapping changes are security-relevant product changes.

- deny → allow is a privilege expansion
- allow → deny is a privilege reduction

Mapping changes require intentional review and regression validation.

## Entitlement Boundary

Module entitlement and User authorization remain separate concerns.

Conceptually:

```text
Entitlement
→ may this Organization use the module?

Authorization
→ may this Membership/User perform this action?
```

DEC-004 does not define:

- billing
- packaging
- subscription
- entitlement implementation

## Reasoning

The Product Model revision changes the Foundation profiles but does not invalidate capability-oriented authorization.

Capability-oriented authorization remains the primary mechanism preventing Owner/Admin semantics from becoming universal Business Module authority.

## Assumptions

- Owner/Admin/Member are sufficient for Phase 1 Foundation responsibilities.
- Kijflow defines authorization policy.
- Business Module requirements will define future module-specific capabilities.
- Default-deny behavior is preferable to accidental privilege expansion.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Existing authorization direction remains usable.
- Owner/Admin do not become automatic superusers.
- Authorization remains testable and explicit.
- Foundation and Business Module responsibilities remain separated.
- No customer-facing permission-configuration complexity is introduced.

### Sacrifices

- Three profile mappings must now be maintained.
- Future Business Module authorization still requires additional validated decisions.
- Mapping growth must be monitored carefully.

## Risks

- Capability catalogs may become too granular.
- Foundation may accumulate Business Module concerns.
- Profile-name checks may bypass capability architecture.
- Ownership semantics may become an informal bypass.
- Static mappings may become difficult to understand as modules grow.

## Deferred

This decision does not define:

- exact Sales capabilities
- exact capabilities for future Business Modules
- Business Module roles
- customer-defined roles
- direct per-user overrides
- shared/cross-module capability ownership
- record/resource-level authorization
- workflow/state authorization
- entitlement implementation

## Reversibility

Moderate.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider if:

- Foundation mappings become excessively large,
- validated Business Modules require materially different authority levels,
- Organizations genuinely require configurable roles,
- per-user exceptions become common,
- resource ownership becomes a frequent authorization dimension,
- contextual/workflow authorization becomes common,
- ad-hoc authorization flags accumulate,
- or entitlement and authorization become improperly coupled.

## Participants

- Founder
- Tech Lead / Software Architect
- QA / Quality Engineer
- Command Center

## Founder Approval

Accepted — Revalidated

# DEC-005 — Phase 1 Tenant Isolation Enforcement

## Original Date

2026-10-08

## Revalidation / Revision Date

2026-10-10

## Decision

The core DEC-005 architecture remains accepted.

Kijflow Phase 1 enforces tenant isolation through defense in depth on a shared PostgreSQL database and shared schema.

The enforcement model combines:

- trusted Organization context
- explicit application/service tenant scoping
- effective PostgreSQL Row-Level Security
- independently enforceable same-tenant relationship integrity
- fail-closed tenant semantics
- preservation of tenant ownership across derived storage boundaries

Organization remains the tenant/data-isolation boundary.

## Context

The original DEC-005 assumed that each authenticated User belonged to exactly one Organization.

Revised DEC-002 allows a global User Identity to hold Memberships in multiple Organizations.

This does not invalidate Organization-as-tenant or the defense-in-depth isolation architecture.

It changes how trusted Organization context must be established.

## Problem

How should Kijflow preserve strict Organization isolation when an authenticated User may legitimately belong to several Organizations?

## Options Considered

1. Trust Organization ID supplied by the authenticated User.
2. Bind each login session permanently to one Organization.
3. Establish Organization context only after validating the authenticated User's usable Membership.
4. Replace Organization-as-tenant with a higher-level tenant account.
5. Replace shared-database RLS architecture with separate databases.

## Selected Option

Use option 3 while preserving the accepted defense-in-depth architecture.

## Revised Context Establishment

Conceptually:

```text
Authenticated User Identity
        ↓
Organization selection intent
        ↓
Validate usable Membership
        ↓
Trusted Organization Context
        ↓
Application / service tenant scoping
        ↓
Effective PostgreSQL RLS
        ↓
Same-tenant relationship integrity
```

A client-supplied Organization identifier is selection intent only.

It does not independently establish trusted tenant context.

## Tenant-Isolation Invariants

1. Organization is the tenant/data-isolation boundary.

2. Tenant context derives only from trusted authenticated or trusted system state.

3. Arbitrary client Organization identifiers never independently establish tenant security context.

4. Missing, conflicting, ambiguous, stale, or untrusted tenant context fails closed.

5. Every tenant-owned resource resolves deterministically to exactly one Organization.

6. Derived tenant ownership is permitted only when mandatory, stable, unambiguous, deterministic, and simple enough to enforce consistently.

7. All normal Kijflow relational access to tenant-owned data remains subject to effective PostgreSQL RLS.

8. Any RLS-bypass execution context is privileged system context and cannot be selected, inherited, or used by ordinary tenant-facing execution.

9. Reused database execution context must never leak tenant identity between tenant-scoped units of work.

10. Application/service data access explicitly operates within trusted Organization context.

11. Read and write operations remain constrained to the trusted Organization.

12. Relationships between tenant-owned resources preserve same-Organization ownership.

13. Same-tenant relationship integrity must be enforceable independently of ordinary application caller discipline.

14. Rejected tenant-inconsistent mutations must not leave partially committed tenant-inconsistent state.

15. Ordinary application operations cannot reassign tenant ownership.

16. Owner never bypasses tenant isolation.

17. Admin never bypasses tenant isolation.

18. Owner/Admin status does not imply system-wide or cross-tenant authority.

19. Cross-tenant/system execution is a separate privileged security boundary.

20. Tenant-scoped non-request execution independently establishes trusted tenant context.

21. Every stored data/resource category receives deliberate ownership classification.

22. Missing or unspecified ownership never implicitly means global/shared.

23. Foundation/system/security data is classified according to actual semantics and is not automatically global.

24. Tenant ownership follows copies and derived representations across caches, files, indexes, exports, and other storage boundaries.

25. Tenant-isolation failures fail closed.

## Multi-Organization Membership Invariants

26. Every tenant-scoped operation operates under exactly one trusted Organization context.

27. A normal authenticated User must have a usable Membership in the selected Organization before trusted Organization context is established.

28. Disabled Membership cannot establish normal tenant access.

29. Revoked/Former Membership cannot establish normal tenant access.

30. Organization switching requires Membership validation for the destination Organization.

31. Stale Membership or stale Organization context fails closed.

32. Organization A tenant context must never be reused as Organization B context.

33. Membership revocation or access reduction must affect subsequent access decisions once authoritative state has changed.

34. A User's Membership-discovery path must not permit enumeration of another User's Memberships.

35. Pre-tenant Foundation operations must not expose tenant-owned Business Module data.

## Pre-Tenant Foundation Access

Some authenticated operations necessarily occur before trusted Organization context exists.

Example:

```text
Authenticated User
→ Which Organizations may this User access?
```

Such operations must use explicitly defined Foundation security rules.

They must only expose information the authenticated User is authorized to discover.

They must not become a generic cross-tenant data-access mechanism.

## Data Classification

Stored information must be classified according to actual ownership/security semantics.

### Tenant-Owned

Owned by exactly one Organization.

### Platform / Global

Intentionally global and not owned by a particular Organization.

Global User Identity is not owned by an Organization.

This does not make all User-related information globally readable.

### Foundation / Security

Classification depends on actual semantics.

Membership is Foundation security data associated with both a User and an Organization.

Access to Membership information must be explicitly constrained.

Missing ownership/classification is an architecture defect, not evidence that information is globally accessible.

## Authentication, Tenant Isolation, and Authorization

These remain separate gates.

Conceptually:

```text
Authenticated
AND usable Membership validated
AND trusted Organization established
AND target belongs to that Organization
AND required authorization capability granted
→ operation may proceed
```

Possessing a capability never expands the tenant boundary.

## Privileged System Execution

Legitimate system-wide or cross-tenant operations must use a separate privileged security boundary.

Such privilege must not:

- come from Owner status
- come from Admin status
- be selected through ordinary request parameters
- be activated through arbitrary Organization IDs
- be inherited accidentally from tenant-scoped execution

The exact privileged mechanism remains deferred.

## Threat Model Additions

Multi-Organization testing must explicitly consider:

- selecting an Organization without Membership
- disabled Membership attempting access
- revoked Membership attempting access
- stale context after Membership revocation
- switching Organization A → Organization B
- Organization-ID manipulation
- another User's Membership enumeration
- database-context leakage during Organization switching
- Owner attempting cross-tenant access
- Admin attempting cross-tenant access

## Maintainability Constraint

Tenant-isolation implementation should continue to favor a small number of standardized, repeatable patterns.

Multi-Organization Membership must not produce separate bespoke tenant-security mechanisms for every feature or module.

## Deferred Implementation Decisions

DEC-005 still does not determine:

- exact RLS policies
- PostgreSQL roles
- `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY`
- database tenant-context mechanism
- active Organization context representation
- JWT/session representation
- FastAPI middleware design
- connection-pool mechanics
- transaction mechanics
- exact database structures required specifically for tenant-isolation enforcement
- exact database enforcement technique for ownership lifecycle beyond the approved Milestone 2 persistence invariants
- cache-key structure
- file/object-storage paths
- queue-message format
- search-index partitioning
- API `403` versus `404`
- privileged maintenance tooling

These remain implementation-design decisions.

## Reasoning

Multi-Organization Membership changes trusted context establishment but does not invalidate Organization-as-tenant or the accepted defense-in-depth architecture.

The primary new requirement is proving usable Membership before establishing tenant context and safely handling Organization switching, disabling, and revocation.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Existing strong database isolation remains.
- Multi-Organization identity can coexist with strict Organization boundaries.
- Authorization and tenant isolation remain separate.
- Forgotten application predicates still have a database backstop.
- Security behavior remains explicit and testable.

### Sacrifices

- Context establishment becomes more complex.
- Pre-tenant Foundation access requires explicit design.
- Organization switching increases security-test scope.
- Database execution context remains security-sensitive.

## Risks

- stale tenant context
- stale Membership state
- tenant-context leakage through pooled connections
- incorrect RLS policies
- Membership enumeration
- unsafe Organization switching
- accidental Owner/Admin bypass
- incomplete ownership classification
- bespoke isolation behavior accumulating across modules

## Reversibility

Moderate.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider if:

- Organization stops being the correct tenant boundary,
- multi-company functionality requires shared tenant business data,
- customers require dedicated databases,
- regulatory requirements require physical isolation,
- tenant-specific data residency becomes required,
- RLS becomes operationally unmanageable,
- standardized isolation patterns cannot be maintained,
- or security review identifies bypass paths that cannot reasonably be controlled.

# DEC-006 — Business Module Independence Principle

## Date

2026-10-11

## Decision

Kijflow Business Modules follow a principle of functional and commercial independence with optional integration.

Each Business Module should be usable for its own core business workflow without requiring the customer to purchase or activate another Business Module.

A Business Module may depend on:

- the Kijflow SaaS Foundation,
- deliberately shared business concepts that are not themselves paid Business Modules,
- and explicit integration interfaces where cross-module workflows provide additional value.

Integration between Business Modules should enhance or extend functionality.

Integration should not be required merely to fill an essential gap in a module's own core workflow.

The default technical architecture for Business Modules is a modular monolith.

Module independence does not require independent deployment, independent databases, microservices, or distributed infrastructure.

## Context

Kijflow is intended to be a modular business-management SaaS.

The Founder wants customers to be able to use only the Business Modules relevant to them.

For example, a customer using Sales Management should be able to complete the core Sales workflow without being required to purchase Inventory, CRM, Accounting, or another Business Module.

At the same time, multiple modules should be able to integrate when the combined workflow provides meaningful business value.

The previous product direction established modularity but intentionally left the exact technical and commercial meaning of "module independence" unresolved.

The project also operates under a one-person software-house constraint.

A strict interpretation of independence could create unnecessary:

- duplicated data,
- duplicated business concepts,
- synchronization logic,
- integration complexity,
- operational overhead,
- and maintenance burden.

A practical boundary is therefore required.

## Problem

How should Kijflow define Business Module independence so that:

- customers are not forced to purchase unrelated modules,
- individual modules remain useful on their own,
- modules can still integrate,
- shared concepts do not become unnecessarily duplicated,
- and the architecture remains maintainable by one person?

## Options Considered

1. Make every Business Module completely self-contained, including duplicate copies of any data or concepts it needs.

2. Use functional and commercial module independence within a modular monolith, while allowing carefully chosen shared business concepts and explicit optional integrations.

3. Build a tightly coupled suite where Business Modules may routinely require other paid Business Modules.

4. Enforce technical independence through separate services, databases, deployment units, or a generic plugin architecture.

## Selected Option

Use option 2.

Kijflow will use:

- functional independence,
- commercial independence by default,
- clear module boundaries,
- carefully controlled shared business concepts,
- optional cross-module integration,
- and a modular-monolith technical architecture.

## Functional Independence

A Business Module must provide a coherent core workflow when used by itself.

Conceptually:

```text
Kijflow Foundation
        +
Selected Business Module
        ↓
Complete core workflow for that module
```

A Business Module must not require another paid Business Module merely to perform functionality that belongs to its own core responsibility.

Example principle:

```text
Sales Management only
        ↓
Core Sales workflow works
```

The customer should not be forced to purchase Inventory or CRM merely so Sales Management can perform its basic Sales responsibilities.

## Commercial Independence

Business Modules should be capable of being offered or entitled independently by default.

A customer should not be forced to subscribe to unrelated Business Modules merely because another module has a technical integration with them.

This decision establishes the commercial independence principle.

It does not determine:

- pricing,
- packaging,
- bundles,
- discounts,
- exact subscription mechanics,
- or entitlement implementation.

Those remain deferred.

A future validated business reason may justify a bundle or dependency, but such dependency must be intentional rather than an accidental consequence of technical architecture.

## Optional Integration

Business Modules may integrate when integration adds meaningful business value.

Conceptually:

```text
Module A
   │
   └──── optional integration ────→ Module B
```

Integration should enhance or automate workflows.

It should not repair an incomplete standalone module.

Examples of valid future integration may include:

- Sales triggering Inventory-related behavior,
- Sales sharing relevant customer context with CRM,
- Purchase affecting Inventory,
- Sales producing information consumed by Accounting.

These examples illustrate integration direction only.

They do not define the actual scope or ownership of those future modules.

## Shared Business Concepts

Business Modules may use deliberately shared business concepts where sharing is justified by actual domain requirements.

A shared business concept:

- is not automatically a paid Business Module,
- should represent genuinely shared business meaning,
- should prevent harmful duplication where appropriate,
- must have clear ownership and responsibility,
- must not become a dumping ground for unrelated functionality.

This decision does not yet determine which concepts belong in a shared business layer.

For example, Customer or Product/Service may later prove to be genuinely shared concepts, but their ownership must be derived from validated Business Module workflows rather than assumed in advance.

Do not create a large speculative Shared Core before those requirements exist.

## Technical Architecture

The default technical architecture for Business Modules is a modular monolith.

Conceptually:

```text
Kijflow Application
│
├── SaaS Foundation
│
├── Shared business concepts
│   └── only where validated
│
├── Sales
├── Inventory
├── CRM
├── Purchase
├── POS
└── Accounting
```

This means modules may exist within:

- the same application,
- the same deployment,
- and the same PostgreSQL system,

while still maintaining clear logical boundaries.

Module independence therefore does not mean deployment independence.

## Module Boundary Principles

Each Business Module should have:

- a clear business responsibility,
- deliberate ownership of module-specific behavior,
- deliberate ownership of module-specific data,
- understandable public/application interfaces,
- limited unnecessary coupling,
- explicit integration points.

One module should not directly depend on another module's internal implementation details.

Cross-module behavior should pass through deliberately defined boundaries.

The exact interface mechanism should follow validated workflow requirements.

## Integration Mechanism

This decision does not select one universal integration mechanism.

Depending on actual requirements, integrations may later use:

- direct application/service interfaces,
- internal APIs,
- domain/application events,
- or another sufficiently simple mechanism.

The simplest mechanism that preserves the required module boundary should be preferred.

Kijflow should not introduce:

- a generic event bus,
- plugin framework,
- message broker,
- service mesh,
- or distributed integration architecture

merely to satisfy an abstract definition of modularity.

## One-Person Maintainability Constraint

Module independence must reduce coupling without creating excessive operational or cognitive complexity.

For Kijflow, maintainability takes priority over architectural purity.

The design should favor:

```text
clear boundaries
+ one application
+ one operational model
+ deliberate integration
```

over:

```text
many services
+ duplicated concepts
+ synchronization
+ distributed infrastructure
```

unless future evidence justifies the additional complexity.

## Non-Goals

DEC-006 does not require:

- absolute independence between all concepts,
- duplicate Customer data per module,
- duplicate Product data per module,
- separate databases per module,
- microservices,
- separately deployed modules,
- generic plugin architecture,
- event-driven architecture for every integration,
- generic integration infrastructure,
- final shared-business-core design,
- final data ownership for Customer,
- final data ownership for Product/Service,
- final Sales module boundaries,
- final Inventory module boundaries,
- final CRM module boundaries,
- final commercial packaging,
- final pricing.

These remain subject to validated Business Module requirements.

## Reasoning

Strict technical independence sounds attractive but would create disproportionate complexity for a one-person software house.

If every module owned independent copies of common concepts, Kijflow would eventually need to solve:

- duplication,
- synchronization,
- conflicting source-of-truth data,
- integration failure,
- data reconciliation,
- and additional testing.

At the opposite extreme, tightly coupling every Business Module would undermine the modular product model and force customers to purchase functionality they may not need.

Functional and commercial independence inside a modular monolith provides a practical middle ground.

It allows:

- standalone customer value,
- optional integration,
- clear domain boundaries,
- lower operational complexity,
- and gradual architecture evolution based on real requirements.

## Trade-offs

### Gains

- Customers can adopt relevant modules without unnecessary module dependencies.
- Each module must provide meaningful standalone value.
- Cross-module integration remains possible.
- Deployment and operations remain simple.
- Shared concepts can prevent unnecessary duplication.
- Architecture remains practical for one-person development.
- Module boundaries can evolve from actual business workflows.

### Sacrifices

- Modules are not technically isolated deployment units.
- Some shared concepts may create controlled coupling.
- Clear ownership decisions will still be required as Business Modules are designed.
- Optional integrations introduce additional test combinations.
- Commercial independence may occasionally conflict with future validated business dependencies.

## Risks

- Shared business concepts may grow into an uncontrolled "shared everything" layer.
- Hidden coupling may develop between modules.
- A module may appear independent while depending indirectly on another module's implementation.
- Developers may duplicate data simply to preserve artificial independence.
- Too many optional integrations may increase testing and maintenance burden.
- Commercial packaging may later require exceptions to the default independence principle.
- Prematurely defining shared entities could lock Kijflow into incorrect domain boundaries.

## Guardrails

To control these risks:

- do not create shared concepts without validated cross-module meaning,
- do not make another paid Business Module a dependency without validated business justification,
- do not duplicate important business concepts merely to claim module independence,
- do not access another module's internals directly,
- do not introduce distributed infrastructure solely for modularity,
- prefer simple explicit integration mechanisms,
- validate module boundaries against real workflows.

## Reversibility

Moderate.

The modular-monolith implementation can evolve later if validated scale, operational, organizational, or isolation requirements justify stronger technical separation.

The commercial independence principle may also be revised for a specific module when validated business requirements demonstrate a genuine dependency.

Such exceptions must be deliberate and documented.

## Reconsideration Trigger

Reconsider this decision if:

- a validated Business Module cannot provide a coherent standalone workflow without another module,
- a genuine business dependency makes independent entitlement misleading,
- the shared business layer becomes excessively large or poorly owned,
- cross-module integrations become difficult to understand or maintain,
- module coupling prevents independent product evolution,
- scale or operational requirements justify independent deployment,
- regulatory or customer requirements justify stronger technical isolation,
- or the modular-monolith approach becomes materially harder to maintain than an alternative.
